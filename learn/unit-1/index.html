Unit I · CO1 · Discuss the fundamentals of entrepreneurship

Entrepreneurship

Entrepreneurship is a system, not a personality trait. A founder or team, an evidence-backed opportunity, a supportive or hostile environment and an organisation that can deliver repeatedly all have to work together.

This unit builds the vocabulary you will use for the rest of the course. Know the difference between an idea, an invention and an opportunity. Know how entrepreneurs are classified, which factors help or block them, and why entrepreneurship matters to the economy.

Case bank

Short Indian cases to quote in answers. Name the case, state what it shows, and link it to the concept.

  • India startup ecosystem snapshot

    Startup India reported 1 lakh-plus recognised startups, 110-plus unicorns, 57 simplified regulations, startups in 670 districts and 12 lakh-plus self-reported jobs as of 31 December 2023.

    Use it for: Entrepreneurship as an ecosystem activity, economic development, environment and growth (I.1, I.8, I.9).

  • Greenviro Global Pvt. Ltd., Coimbatore

    Converts coconut agricultural waste into charcoal and briquettes. Local waste becomes a revenue-earning product with environmental value.

    Use it for: Characteristics, types and mindset. A clear example of innovation, local opportunity recognition and social value in one venture (I.2, I.3, I.5).

  • Breathe Applied Sciences Pvt. Ltd.

    Works on converting CO₂ into purified methanol and high-energy-density chemicals.

    Use it for: Inventor versus entrepreneur. The science is the invention, but the venture must also solve cost, process, customer and regulatory problems (I.6).

  • Quixy (Vividminds Technologies)

    A no-code platform that lets business users build enterprise applications with drag-and-drop workflows.

    Use it for: Company versus startup. What makes it a startup is the search for a repeatable, scalable model, not the date it was registered (I.7).

2-mark questions with model answers

Write your answer first. A 2-mark answer needs a precise definition or two clear points, not a paragraph.

Define the term entrepreneur.

An entrepreneur is a person who identifies an opportunity, organises the resources needed to pursue it and accepts calculated risk to create value through a new or improved venture. The entrepreneur initiates, innovates and takes responsibility for outcomes under uncertainty.

State the meaning of entrepreneurship.

Entrepreneurship is the process of identifying an opportunity, organising resources, accepting calculated risk and creating economic or social value through a new or improved venture. The entrepreneur is the actor; entrepreneurship is the process.

Name the four stages shown in the textbook model of the entrepreneurial process.

Aurangabadkar and Singh (Fig. 1.5): (1) decision to become an entrepreneur, (2) developing successful business ideas, (3) moving from an idea to an entrepreneurial firm, and (4) managing and growing the entrepreneurial firm.

State the meaning of entrepreneurial risk.

Entrepreneurial risk is the possibility of loss arising from uncertainty about market acceptance, cost, technology, regulation and competition. Entrepreneurs take calculated risk by testing assumptions in small steps before committing major resources.

List any two characteristics of a successful entrepreneur.

Any two, for example (1) creativity and innovation, the ability to generate ideas and turn them into practical value, and (2) calculated risk-taking, acting under uncertainty in measured steps. Persistence, leadership, initiative and ethics are also acceptable.

Define entrepreneurial competency.

Entrepreneurial competency is the combination of knowledge, skills, behaviour and judgement that enables a person to recognise opportunities, mobilise resources and build a venture. Examples include opportunity recognition, customer orientation, persistence and financial awareness.

Name two ethical responsibilities of an entrepreneur.

(1) Report product performance and safety results honestly, and (2) keep fair commitments to customers, workers and investors. Protecting customer data and the environment are also valid answers.

State the meaning of intrapreneurship.

Intrapreneurship is entrepreneurial activity carried out inside an existing organisation. An employee (an intrapreneur) develops a new product, process or venture using the organisation's resources, for example an automobile company's internal EV battery venture.

Define social entrepreneurship.

Social entrepreneurship is the pursuit of a measurable social or environmental mission through a financially sustainable operating model, not through charity alone.

Define necessity-driven entrepreneurship.

Necessity-driven entrepreneurship begins mainly when satisfactory employment or income alternatives are limited, so self-employment becomes a practical livelihood route.

State the meaning of opportunity-driven entrepreneurship.

Opportunity-driven entrepreneurship begins mainly when a person identifies an attractive possibility to create value, solve a problem, gain independence or pursue growth.

List two internal factors affecting entrepreneurial growth.

(1) The founder's motivation and need for achievement, and (2) the team's skills and capability. Risk attitude, confidence and execution discipline are also acceptable.

List two external factors affecting entrepreneurial growth.

(1) Access to finance, and (2) market demand. Infrastructure, government policy and regulation, and social attitudes are also acceptable.

Name any two motives for becoming an entrepreneur.

(1) Achievement and independence, being one's own boss, and (2) financial reward or wealth creation. Applying one's knowledge, solving a meaningful problem, social impact and limited employment options are also valid.

Define innovation in an entrepreneurial context.

Innovation is the practical introduction of a new or significantly improved product, process, market or business model that creates value for customers or society. An invention becomes an innovation only when it is implemented and used.

Distinguish an inventor from an entrepreneur.

An inventor creates a new product, process or technical solution. An entrepreneur recognises an opportunity and organises resources (customers, finance, production, team) to turn an idea or invention into a venture that creates and captures value. One person can be both.

State the meaning of commercialisation.

Commercialisation is the process of turning an invention or idea into a product or service that customers buy, through validation, design for manufacture, pricing, distribution, support and protection of the IP.

State the meaning of a startup.

A startup is a young venture that searches for a repeatable, scalable and sustainable business model under high uncertainty. It tests assumptions rather than executing a known model.

Name any two participants in an entrepreneurial ecosystem.

(1) Incubators and accelerators, and (2) investors and banks. Mentors, universities and labs, government agencies, customers and suppliers are also acceptable.

Define an entrepreneurial ecosystem.

An entrepreneurial ecosystem is the connected set of people, institutions, resources, culture, markets and policies that support the birth, survival and growth of ventures, such as founders, incubators, investors, universities and government bodies.

List any two stages in the growth of an entrepreneurial firm.

For example, (1) start-up or validation, testing the model with early customers, and (2) growth or scaling, expanding capacity, team and market. Later stages include expansion and maturity. Using the textbook process model, "moving from an idea to a firm" and "managing and growing the firm" are also valid.

List two contributions of entrepreneurship to economic development.

(1) Employment generation, through direct and indirect jobs, and (2) capital formation, by mobilising savings into productive investment. Innovation, balanced regional development and export promotion are also acceptable.

14-mark questions

Plan before you write: an introduction, the points below as headings with explanation and an example, then a short conclusion. Open a question to see the points to cover.

1Define the terms entrepreneur and entrepreneurship, and explain the difference between them. (Aurangabadkar & Singh, p. 1.48)

Points to cover

  • Define the entrepreneur (the actor) and entrepreneurship (the process) in one precise sentence each.
  • Roles of the entrepreneur, who visualises, initiates, organises, innovates, takes risk and is responsible for outcomes.
  • The four elements of entrepreneurship, which are opportunity, resources, risk and value creation.
  • Differences in a table covering person versus process, who acts versus how value is created, and examples.
  • Engineering example, such as the low-cost interlocking blocks tested with builders.
  • Conclude that the two are inseparable, because the process needs the actor and the actor creates value only through the process.

Study: I.1 Concept of entrepreneurship

2Explain the relationship among entrepreneur, entrepreneurship and enterprise.

Points to cover

  • The entrepreneur is the person or team who perceives the opportunity and bears risk.
  • Entrepreneurship is the process of identifying an opportunity, organising resources and creating value.
  • The enterprise is the organisation or venture created, through which value is delivered repeatedly.
  • The relationship is that the entrepreneur, through entrepreneurship, creates and runs the enterprise. Show it as a flow diagram: person → process → organisation.
  • Use the new-venture system of environment, founding team, venture concept and organisation (Allen).
  • Illustrate with one venture, such as Greenviro, showing all three.

3Describe the characteristics, competencies and roles of a successful entrepreneur.

Points to cover

  • Characteristics such as creativity, innovation, initiative, leadership, persistence, calculated risk-taking and commitment.
  • Competencies, which combine knowledge, skill, behaviour and judgement (for example opportunity recognition, customer orientation, resource mobilisation, communication and financial awareness).
  • Roles of the entrepreneur, who visualises, initiates, organises, innovates, plans, motivates and takes responsibility.
  • Technical judgement for engineers covering manufacturability, serviceability, certification and IP.
  • Ethics as a characteristic, meaning honest reporting and trust.
  • Limits such as overconfidence and escalation of commitment, with an example like the seed-sowing robot team.

4Explain the economic and non-economic factors affecting entrepreneurial growth.

Points to cover

  • Economic factors, which are capital, labour, raw materials, infrastructure, technology and market demand. Explain each briefly.
  • Social factors, which are family support, community values, role models, status of business and networks.
  • Psychological factors, which are need for achievement, confidence, risk attitude and persistence.
  • Government and political factors, which are policy, regulation, incentives, training and institutional support.
  • Show how the factors interact. A good idea fails if finance or regulation is ignored.
  • Example such as the agricultural drone startup.

5Discuss the contribution of entrepreneurship to employment, innovation, competition, regional development and social value.

Points to cover

  • Employment, both direct and indirect jobs, since large firms cannot absorb all youth.
  • Innovation, meaning new products and processes, higher productivity and creative destruction.
  • Competition, which improves quality and price for customers and keeps incumbents efficient.
  • Regional development, through rural and semi-urban enterprises and clusters that reduce imbalance.
  • Social value, through inclusive entrepreneurship and social enterprises.
  • Evidence from the Startup India snapshot (670 districts, 12 lakh-plus jobs), and a caution about displacement and transition costs.

6Explain innovation-driven, necessity-driven, social and intrapreneurial forms of entrepreneurship with suitable examples.

Points to cover

  • Innovation-driven ventures commercialise a novel solution, often scalable (example Breathe Applied Sciences).
  • Necessity-driven ventures start for livelihood when alternatives are limited (example a home food business started after job loss).
  • Social entrepreneurship pursues a measurable mission with sustainable revenue (example Greenviro's environmental value).
  • Intrapreneurship builds a new venture inside an existing firm (example an automaker's EV battery unit).
  • Compare their triggers, objectives and support needs in a table.
  • Note that motives are mixed and ventures can move between forms.

Study: I.3 Types of entrepreneurship, I.7 Companies and startups

7Describe the roles of mentors, incubators, finance providers, customers, laboratories and public agencies in an entrepreneurial ecosystem.

Points to cover

  • Mentors provide experience, judgement and networks.
  • Incubators provide workspace, structured programmes, mentoring and investor links.
  • Finance providers are banks (debt), angels and VCs (equity) and grant agencies.
  • Customers give early feedback, pilots and the first revenue that validates the venture.
  • Laboratories and universities provide testing, certification support, talent and knowledge.
  • Public agencies provide policy, registration, schemes and procurement. Conclude that the actors must be matched to the venture's stage.

Study: I.8 Entrepreneurial environment and growth

8Explain entrepreneurial uncertainty, risk, validation and staged commitment.

Points to cover

  • Uncertainty means incomplete information about market, technology, cost and regulation.
  • Risk is the possible loss from that uncertainty, which entrepreneurs manage by taking calculated risk.
  • Validation means identifying the riskiest assumption, designing the smallest credible test, setting a threshold and collecting evidence.
  • Decision rule is to continue, change, pause or stop.
  • Staged commitment and affordable loss limit each step's exposure.
  • Example of a sensor startup piloting with 10 users before buying production tooling.

Study: I.1 Concept of entrepreneurship, I.5 Entrepreneurship mindset

9(i) Explain the meanings of invention, idea and opportunity.(ii) Illustrate their differences with one engineering-related example.

Points to cover

  • An idea is a possible response to a problem.
  • An invention is a novel technical creation.
  • An opportunity is evidence-supported, with a defined customer, a meaningful need and a way to create and capture value.
  • Compare novelty, evidence and value in a table.
  • Use the water-level sensor example. Idea = "monitor tank levels". Invention = the new low-cost sensor. Opportunity = farmers and households shown to need it and willing to pay, with a service model.

Study: I.1 Concept of entrepreneurship, I.6 Inventors and entrepreneurs

10(i) Explain necessity-driven and opportunity-driven entrepreneurship.(ii) Describe suitable forms of support for each.

Points to cover

  • Compare trigger, first objective, resource position and early priority.
  • Necessity-driven ventures need basic business skills, market linkage, dependable working capital and social protection.
  • Opportunity-driven ventures need incubation, IP advice, specialist talent, networks and risk capital.
  • Both need customer value, ethics and cash discipline.
  • Note that motives can be mixed and change over time.

Study: I.3 Types of entrepreneurship

11(i) Describe the ethical responsibilities of an entrepreneur during product testing.(ii) Explain the likely effects of concealing an unfavourable safety result.

Points to cover

  • Responsibilities include honest reporting of results, user safety and informed consent, data privacy, and not overstating claims.
  • Disclose failures and correct the course before launch.
  • Concealment can cause harm to users and legal liability.
  • It also leads to loss of trust from customers, investors and regulators.
  • It can bring product recall, penalties and collapse of the venture's reputation.
  • Ethics is part of venture quality, not an add-on.

Study: I.2 Characteristics of entrepreneurship, I.5 Entrepreneurship mindset

12(i) Explain how entrepreneurship can produce creative destruction and new economic value.(ii) Illustrate the explanation with an Indian engineering-sector example.

Points to cover

  • Creative destruction is when a superior product, process or model reallocates resources away from older arrangements.
  • New value comes from new jobs, productivity, lower cost and better quality.
  • There are costs as well, such as displaced workers, obsolete skills and transition.
  • Indian example, such as UPI or digital payments changing cash-handling services, or EV component makers displacing some IC-engine parts suppliers.
  • Conclude that responsible entrepreneurship manages the transition.

Study: I.9 Role of entrepreneurship in economic development

13(i) Classify the factors affecting a rural technology venture as internal or external.(ii) Explain how any four of these factors influence entrepreneurial growth.

Points to cover

  • Internal factors are founder skills, motivation, risk attitude and team capability.
  • External factors are finance access, rural infrastructure (power, roads, internet), market demand, social acceptance and government schemes.
  • Explain four factors in detail, each with how it helps or blocks growth.
  • Use an example such as a solar pump service venture in a village.

Study: I.4 Factors affecting entrepreneurs

14(i) Describe the evidence used to establish a promising venture opportunity.(ii) Explain why social-media reach alone is insufficient evidence.

Points to cover

  • Evidence includes problem frequency and severity, customer access, willingness to try or pay, and technical and economic feasibility.
  • Strong signals are behaviour such as pre-orders, deposits, repeat use and signed pilots.
  • Weak signals are likes, impressions, praise from friends and novelty.
  • Reach shows attention, not need or payment. The audience may not be the buyer.
  • Combine several sources of evidence (triangulation).

Study: I.1 Concept of entrepreneurship, I.5 Entrepreneurship mindset

15(i) Explain the stages of opportunity validation for a student venture.(ii) Describe how staged commitments limit time and capital risk.

Points to cover

  • Stages: observe the problem, frame the opportunity, map assumptions, run the smallest credible test, pilot, then decide.
  • Each stage needs a threshold and a decision to continue, change, pause or stop.
  • Staged commitment releases time and money only after the previous evidence justifies it.
  • Affordable loss caps exposure at each step.
  • Example of a campus chatbot tested for weekly use before building advanced features.
