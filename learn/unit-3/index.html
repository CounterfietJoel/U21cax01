Unit III · CO3 · Assess business plan and business pitch as entrepreneurial tools

Business Plan and Pitch

A business plan is a connected argument, not a set of optimistic sections. This unit covers what a plan is for, how it is written, evaluated and implemented, the marketing, financial and organisational plans inside it, and how the plan is compressed into an investor pitch.

Hold on to three ideas. Consistency across sections is the main quality test. Profit is not cash, so the cash-flow statement matters as much as the income statement. A pitch ends with a precise ask tied to milestones.

2-mark questions with model answers

Write your answer first. A 2-mark answer needs a precise definition or two clear points, not a paragraph.

Explain how a business plan serves both the entrepreneur and external stakeholders.

For the entrepreneur, the plan is a thinking tool. It tests logic, exposes assumptions, coordinates decisions and sets milestones. For external stakeholders such as investors, lenders, suppliers and partners, it is a communication tool that lets them judge credibility, risk, resource need and return before committing.

Distinguish an assumption, evidence and an aspiration in a business plan.

An assumption is a belief the plan depends on but has not yet tested, for example a 5 percent conversion rate. Evidence is something observed or measured, such as 20 paid pilot orders. An aspiration is a target the team hopes to reach, such as market leadership in three years. A credible plan labels each one honestly.

Summarise the information that belongs in the executive summary of a business plan.

The executive summary concisely states the problem, the proposed solution, the target customer and market, the evidence of demand, the business model, the competitive advantage, the team, the headline financial logic, the next milestone and the funding requirement or ask. It is written last but placed first.

Explain the purpose of initial screening and the two terminate decisions in a project-feasibility study.

Initial screening asks whether the idea is prima facie worthwhile. If not, the project is terminated before money is spent on a detailed study. After the feasibility plan and financial analysis, a second decision asks whether the project is worthwhile. If not, it is terminated before a funding proposal is prepared. Both stop weak projects before commitment escalates (Charantimath, Fig. 5.2).

Use the owner-budget-metric-date format to convert a launch objective into a milestone.

Objective: launch the product. Milestone: the operations lead (owner) completes 20 paid pilot installations (metric) with less than 2 percent critical failure by 30 September (date), within ₹4 lakh (budget).

Explain how an implementation plan converts decisions into action.

It breaks each decision into milestones and assigns each one an owner, a budget, a metric, a due date and its dependencies. Progress is reviewed at a fixed rhythm against the metrics, and a response rule says what corrective action follows when a milestone slips.

Explain the main components of a marketing plan.

Segmentation divides the market into meaningful groups. Targeting picks the first segment. Positioning states why the offer is different and valuable. The marketing mix sets product, price, place and promotion. The sales process and retention cover acquisition, service, trust and repeat value. Metrics such as conversion and CAC track progress.

Use a customer-segment statement to specify two suitable acquisition metrics.

Segment: private dental clinics in Coimbatore with two or more chairs. Metric 1: lead-to-customer conversion, which is clinics signed divided by qualified clinic leads. Metric 2: CAC, which is sales and demo cost divided by clinics signed in the quarter.

Rewrite the claim 'everyone will buy our product' as a testable, evidence-based statement.

For example: 'At least 15 of 60 qualified hostel residents contacted on our campus will pre-order the product at ₹499 within four weeks.' It names a segment, a price, a measurable conversion and a time limit, so it can be tested and proved wrong.

Calculate contribution per unit when selling price is ₹500 and variable cost is ₹320.

Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost = 500 − 320 = ₹180. Each unit sold adds ₹180 toward fixed cost and profit.

Two machines have fixed and variable costs of ₹100 and ₹2, and ₹200 and ₹1, per piece. The price is ₹3.50 for the first 800 units and ₹3.00 after. Find each break-even quantity and its use.

Machine A has a contribution of 3.50 − 2 = ₹1.50, so break-even = 100 ÷ 1.50 = 66.67, or 67 units. Machine B has a contribution of 3.50 − 1 = ₹2.50, so break-even = 200 ÷ 2.50 = 80 units. Both are under 800 units, so the price change does not affect them. In a financial plan, break-even shows the minimum volume needed and helps compare options. A breaks even sooner, but B earns more per unit afterwards.

Monthly sales are 70, 68, 82 and 95 units. Using exponential smoothing with α = 0.4 and the first observation as the initial forecast, estimate next month and state one caution.

F2 = 70. F3 = 0.4(68) + 0.6(70) = 69.2. F4 = 0.4(82) + 0.6(69.2) = 74.32. F5 = 0.4(95) + 0.6(74.32) = 82.59, about 82.6 units. Caution — smoothing lags a rising trend and ignores seasonality, shocks and capacity limits, so check it against market and operational evidence.

Distinguish cash flow from accounting profit.

Profit is revenue minus expenses recognised under accounting rules for a period, including non-cash items such as depreciation. Cash flow is the actual money received and paid, and when. A firm can show a profit and still run out of cash if customers pay late or inventory and equipment are paid for first.

Explain the purpose of the projected income statement, cash-flow statement and balance sheet.

The income statement tests whether the operating model earns an acceptable margin over a period. The cash-flow statement tests whether the venture can pay its obligations when due and shows the month cash may run short. The balance sheet shows what the venture owns, owes and how it is financed at a given date.

Apply sensitivity analysis to show the direction of profit change when selling price falls and unit sales remain constant.

If the price falls and volume and variable cost per unit are unchanged, revenue falls by the price drop × units, while variable and fixed costs stay the same. Contribution per unit and profit therefore fall by the same amount, and the break-even quantity rises.

Use base, upside and downside labels to organise three stated sales forecasts.

For forecasts of 800, 1,000 and 1,300 units: the downside is 800 (lower conversion, delayed receipts), the base is 1,000 (the most likely stated assumptions) and the upside is 1,300 (higher conversion or repeat purchase). Each label should list the assumption that differs from the base.

Explain the purpose of an organisational plan.

It shows who will do the work and who is accountable. It sets out founder roles and ownership, core-team responsibilities, advisors, the hiring roadmap by stage and governance policies, and it identifies capability gaps. Investors use it to judge whether the team can execute the plan.

Explain the problem-solution-evidence sequence in an investor pitch.

The pitch first establishes a specific, important customer problem, then presents the solution and its value to that customer, and then shows evidence such as pilots, paying users, letters of intent or trial results proving that the solution works and is wanted. Each step builds the listener's confidence before the market, model and ask.

Explain what a clear investor ask should contain.

The amount required, the instrument where relevant (equity, convertible note or debt), the use of funds broken into heads, the milestone each use will achieve, the metric that will prove it and the effect on runway, for example ₹30 lakh for 12 months to reach 60 paying customers.

Explain why financial assumptions must be disclosed honestly in an investor pitch.

Investors price risk using the assumptions. Hidden or inflated assumptions mislead them, can create legal and reputational damage, and destroy trust when actual results diverge. Disclosing assumptions with base, upside and downside cases shows the founder understands the business and lets both sides agree milestones and terms realistically.

14-mark questions

Plan before you write: an introduction, the points below as headings with explanation and an example, then a short conclusion. Open a question to see the points to cover.

1Why is a business plan important to the entrepreneur, investor, customer and suppliers? Explain.

Points to cover

  • Define a business plan as both a thinking tool and a communication tool.
  • Entrepreneur — tests logic, exposes assumptions, coordinates decisions, allocates resources and sets milestones.
  • Investor and lender — judge credibility, risk, resource need, return and repayment capacity.
  • Customer — gains confidence in continuity, quality, service and support.
  • Suppliers and key employees — judge order stability, payment reliability and whether to commit.
  • It is a living document, revised as evidence arrives, with an example such as the assistive-device startup.

Study: III.1 Nature of a business plan

2Explain the sections of a complete business plan and the decision purpose of each section.

Points to cover

  • Executive summary, written last and placed first, lets a reader decide whether to read on.
  • Company description, problem, solution and market analysis show a real need and a reachable customer.
  • Competitor analysis and the marketing plan show advantage and acquisition.
  • Operations and organisation plans show delivery capacity and responsibility.
  • The financial plan shows money needed, margin and cash timing.
  • Implementation schedule, risk analysis and appendices show milestones, responses and supporting evidence.

Study: III.2 Writing a business plan

3Analyse the consistency required among the marketing, operations, organisational and financial sections of a business plan.

Points to cover

  • Consistency is the central quality test. Contradictions reveal more than polished prose.
  • Marketing volume must be deliverable by operations capacity, suppliers and quality control.
  • Capacity needs people (organisation plan) and working capital (financial plan).
  • Hiring and equipment timing must appear in the cash flow, and credit terms in receivables.
  • Growth must increase inventory, support and service capacity.
  • Trace one unit of demand from lead to cash collection to test consistency.

Study: III.2 Writing a business plan, III.4 Marketing plan, III.5 Financial plan, III.6 Organisational plan

4Analyse a business plan that projects rapid sales growth without customer evidence, capacity planning, working-capital estimates or risk disclosure.

Points to cover

  • No customer evidence means demand is an aspiration, so it fails market feasibility.
  • No capacity plan means sales cannot be delivered, so it fails operational feasibility.
  • No working-capital estimate hides cash shortages, so a profitable plan may still become insolvent.
  • No risk disclosure misleads readers and damages trust.
  • The fix is pilot evidence, a capacity plan, a monthly cash flow, base, upside and downside scenarios and a risk register.
  • Conclude that the plan should not be funded in its current form.

Study: III.3 Evaluating and implementing a business plan, III.5 Financial plan

5Explain the contents of an organisational plan for a venture moving from prototype to pilot production.

Points to cover

  • Founder roles, ownership and the founder agreement.
  • Core-team owners for product, production, quality, sales, finance and compliance.
  • Capability gaps at pilot stage, such as production, quality assurance, supply chain and regulatory skills.
  • Advisors, mentors and external professionals such as a CA or legal adviser.
  • A hiring roadmap by stage, linked to the cash flow.
  • Policies for quality, safety, data, finance and ethics, with an example table.

Study: III.6 Organisational plan

6Explain a logical investor-pitch sequence from customer problem to funding ask.

Points to cover

  • Problem → target segment → solution and value.
  • Evidence or traction → market (TAM, SAM, SOM) → business model.
  • Competition and advantage → go-to-market → team.
  • Economics and projections with assumptions → risks and milestones.
  • The ask covers the amount, instrument, use of funds, milestone, metric and runway effect.
  • Each element should reduce an important uncertainty for the listener.

Study: III.7 Business pitch, III.8 Preparing an investor presentation

7Analyse the usefulness and limitations of break-even, payback, gross margin, cash runway and sensitivity analysis in an early-stage venture.

Points to cover

  • Break-even (fixed cost ÷ contribution) gives the minimum volume, but assumes constant price and cost and says nothing about timing.
  • Payback (outlay ÷ annual inflow) is simple, but ignores later cash and the time value of money.
  • Gross margin shows pricing and production economics, but ignores fixed cost and cash timing.
  • Runway (cash ÷ monthly burn) gives urgency, but practical runway is shorter with delayed receipts and one-time costs.
  • Sensitivity analysis identifies the critical variables, but its value depends on honest assumptions.
  • Use them together, with worked numbers such as the 2,000-unit break-even and the 6.67-month runway.

Study: III.5 Financial plan

8Explain how milestones, budgets, owners, review routines and corrective action convert a business plan into implementation.

Points to cover

  • Milestones are evidence-producing results, not activities.
  • Budgets allocate money to each milestone.
  • Owners make one person accountable for each result.
  • Review routines check metrics against plan at a fixed rhythm.
  • Corrective action follows a response rule when a metric slips.
  • Give an example such as 20 paid pilots, under 2 percent failure, by 30 September, within ₹4 lakh.

Study: III.3 Evaluating and implementing a business plan

9(i) Analyse the weakness in claiming a large market from evidence collected from only five users.(ii) Apply TAM-SAM-SOM terms to organise a more defensible market estimate.

Points to cover

  • Five users is a tiny, probably friendly sample. It may improve the next assumption but cannot justify a national SOM.
  • Define TAM with explicit customer, geography, need and price boundaries.
  • Narrow to SAM by language, channel, regulation, technology and geography.
  • Estimate SOM bottom-up from reachable customers, conversion, units, repeat rate and price.
  • State the source and date for each input.
  • Plan the next test to grow the evidence.

Study: III.4 Marketing plan

10(i) Use a base-case cash-flow format to organise monthly revenue, cost, working-capital and closing-cash assumptions.(ii) Apply lower-sales, higher-cost and delayed-payment changes to the same format.

Points to cover

  • Lines are opening cash, customer receipts, variable and inventory payments, fixed payments, capital expenditure, finance movements and closing cash.
  • Closing cash = opening cash + inflows − outflows, carried to the next month.
  • Downside — lower conversion reduces receipts, and a payment delay shifts them later.
  • Higher unit cost raises variable payments and the inventory requirement.
  • Do not assume unapproved money. Flag the first negative month or breach of the cash buffer.
  • Use the result to decide deposits, credit limits, inventory, finance timing and a safe growth rate.

Study: III.5 Financial plan

11(i) Analyse an investor pitch that lists product features but omits the customer problem, traction, competition, business model and ask.(ii) Reorder the omitted elements into a logical pitch sequence.

Points to cover

  • Features without a problem give the listener no reason to care.
  • Without traction there is no evidence. Without competition the founder looks naive. Without a model there is no path to money. Without an ask there is no next step.
  • Reordered, the sequence is problem → solution (features as benefits) → traction → market → business model → competition → team → financials → ask.
  • Each element reduces an uncertainty for the investor.
  • Translate features into customer outcomes.
  • Close with a precise ask and next step.

Study: III.7 Business pitch, III.8 Preparing an investor presentation

12(i) Distinguish the purpose of a marketing, operations, organisational and financial plan.(ii) Analyse two assumptions that must remain consistent across the four plans.

Points to cover

  • Marketing covers how customers are reached, acquired and kept.
  • Operations covers how the offering is produced and delivered.
  • Organisation covers who is responsible and which capabilities are needed.
  • Finance covers money needed, margin and cash timing.
  • Assumption 1 — sales volume drives capacity, staff, inventory and cash.
  • Assumption 2 — customer credit terms drive receivables, working capital and runway.

Study: III.2 Writing a business plan, III.4 Marketing plan, III.5 Financial plan, III.6 Organisational plan

13(i) Analyse how a venture showing accounting profit can still become insolvent.(ii) Apply working-capital and cash-control measures to the stated situation.

Points to cover

  • Profit is recognised on sale, but cash arrives on collection.
  • Late receivables, inventory build-up, equipment purchases, debt service and tax drain cash.
  • Rapid growth makes the gap worse.
  • Measures include advance deposits, shorter credit terms, collection follow-up, supplier credit and lean inventory.
  • Keep a cash buffer, a monthly cash forecast and pre-arranged working-capital finance.
  • Grow at a rate the cash can support.

Study: III.5 Financial plan

14(i) Use an amount-purpose-milestone-metric format to state a pilot-production funding ask.(ii) Map each use of funds to its milestone and expected runway effect.

Points to cover

  • The ask states an amount, for example ₹30 lakh, with the instrument.
  • It states the purpose, such as tooling, certification and sales.
  • It names the milestone, for example 200 units and 60 paying customers.
  • It names the metric, for example unit cost, failure rate and repeat orders.
  • Present a table of each use, its amount, its milestone and the months of runway it adds.
  • Close with the next step and the information offered.

Study: III.8 Preparing an investor presentation, III.9 The investment pitch and its delivery

15(i) Analyse the ethical and strategic consequences of presenting only an optimistic financial forecast to investors.(ii) Present the same forecast as base, upside and downside scenarios with explicit assumptions.

Points to cover

  • Ethically it misleads investors, and the founder risks legal and reputational damage.
  • Strategically it builds unrealistic milestones, causes trust to collapse when results miss and leads to poor terms later.
  • Base uses the most likely assumptions with their stated sources.
  • Upside uses higher conversion or repeat purchase and is labelled as such.
  • Downside uses lower volume, higher cost and delayed payment, with a cash-flow effect.
  • Sensitivity analysis shows which variable matters most.
