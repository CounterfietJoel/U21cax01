III.5 Financial plan

You should be able to: Explain basic financial plan components.

  • 📖 Read first: U21CAX01 Study Material, p. 33
  • ⏱ About 10 minutes to revise

1In a nutshell

A financial plan estimates the money needed to start and run the venture, the expected revenue, expenses, profit, cash flow, break-even point and funding requirement. Its components are fixed capital, working capital, sales forecast, cost of goods sold, operating expenses, depreciation, interest, the three projected statements and key ratios.

  1. Profit is not cash. Profit recognises revenue and expense under accounting rules. Cash flow records when money is actually received or paid. A growing venture can show a profit and still become insolvent if customers pay late, inventory builds up or equipment and taxes are paid first.
  2. Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost. Break-even quantity = fixed cost ÷ contribution per unit. At break-even, total contribution equals total fixed cost.
  3. Runway = available cash ÷ monthly net burn. Simple payback = initial outlay ÷ annual net cash inflow.
  4. Early numbers are conditional estimates. Show base, upside and downside scenarios and use sensitivity analysis to see which assumption moves the result most.
  5. Payback is easy to explain but ignores cash after the payback point and the time value of money.

2Building blocks

  • Startup costMachines, tools, registration, deposits and initial marketing.
  • Working capitalInventory, wages, utilities, transport and receivables.
  • Revenue modelUnit price, subscription, commission, service fee or licence.
  • Break-evenFixed cost divided by contribution per unit.
  • Funding mixOwner funds, bank loan, grant, angel, VC or crowdfunding.
The three projected statements
StatementQuestion it answersKey early-venture items
Income statementCan the operating model earn an acceptable margin?Sales, discounts, variable cost, salaries, rent, depreciation, interest and tax
Cash-flow statementCan the venture pay its obligations when due?Collection delay, inventory, equipment, deposits, loans, tax and a cash buffer
Balance sheetWhat does it own and owe at a given date?Cash, receivables, inventory, equipment, payables, debt and owners' funds

3See it applied

Example

Worked example 1 (two machines). Machine A has a fixed cost of ₹100 and a variable cost of ₹2, Machine B a fixed cost of ₹200 and a variable cost of ₹1, and the price is ₹3.50. Contribution is ₹1.50 for A and ₹2.50 for B, so break-even is 100 ÷ 1.50 = 66.67, or 67 units, for A and 200 ÷ 2.50 = 80 units for B. Both are below 800 units, so the lower price after 800 units does not change them. Worked example 2 (contribution and runway). At a price of ₹500 and a variable cost of ₹320, contribution is ₹180. With fixed cost of ₹3,60,000, break-even is 2,000 units. With ₹12,00,000 of cash and a monthly net burn of ₹1,80,000, runway is 6.67 months. Worked example 3 (forecast). Monthly sales of 70, 68, 82 and 95 with α = 0.4 give F3 = 69.2, F4 = 74.32 and F5 = 82.6 units.

4Avoid these mistakes

  • Making wishful revenue projections.
  • Underestimating working capital.
  • Explaining break-even only as a formula instead of in simple units.

5Key terms

Contribution
Selling price minus variable cost per unit, the amount each sale adds toward fixed cost and profit.
Break-even point
The volume at which total contribution equals fixed cost, so operating profit is zero.
Working capital
Money tied up in day-to-day operations such as inventory, receivables and wages.
Cash runway
The number of months the venture can operate on its available cash at the current net burn.
Sensitivity analysis
Changing one or more assumptions to see how much the outcome changes and which variable matters most.

7Exam practice: 2-mark questions

Answer in your own words first, then open the model answer to compare.

Calculate contribution per unit when selling price is ₹500 and variable cost is ₹320.

Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost = 500 − 320 = ₹180. Each unit sold adds ₹180 toward fixed cost and profit.

Two machines have fixed and variable costs of ₹100 and ₹2, and ₹200 and ₹1, per piece. The price is ₹3.50 for the first 800 units and ₹3.00 after. Find each break-even quantity and its use.

Machine A has a contribution of 3.50 − 2 = ₹1.50, so break-even = 100 ÷ 1.50 = 66.67, or 67 units. Machine B has a contribution of 3.50 − 1 = ₹2.50, so break-even = 200 ÷ 2.50 = 80 units. Both are under 800 units, so the price change does not affect them. In a financial plan, break-even shows the minimum volume needed and helps compare options. A breaks even sooner, but B earns more per unit afterwards.

Monthly sales are 70, 68, 82 and 95 units. Using exponential smoothing with α = 0.4 and the first observation as the initial forecast, estimate next month and state one caution.

F2 = 70. F3 = 0.4(68) + 0.6(70) = 69.2. F4 = 0.4(82) + 0.6(69.2) = 74.32. F5 = 0.4(95) + 0.6(74.32) = 82.59, about 82.6 units. Caution — smoothing lags a rising trend and ignores seasonality, shocks and capacity limits, so check it against market and operational evidence.

Distinguish cash flow from accounting profit.

Profit is revenue minus expenses recognised under accounting rules for a period, including non-cash items such as depreciation. Cash flow is the actual money received and paid, and when. A firm can show a profit and still run out of cash if customers pay late or inventory and equipment are paid for first.

Explain the purpose of the projected income statement, cash-flow statement and balance sheet.

The income statement tests whether the operating model earns an acceptable margin over a period. The cash-flow statement tests whether the venture can pay its obligations when due and shows the month cash may run short. The balance sheet shows what the venture owns, owes and how it is financed at a given date.

Apply sensitivity analysis to show the direction of profit change when selling price falls and unit sales remain constant.

If the price falls and volume and variable cost per unit are unchanged, revenue falls by the price drop × units, while variable and fixed costs stay the same. Contribution per unit and profit therefore fall by the same amount, and the break-even quantity rises.

Use base, upside and downside labels to organise three stated sales forecasts.

For forecasts of 800, 1,000 and 1,300 units: the downside is 800 (lower conversion, delayed receipts), the base is 1,000 (the most likely stated assumptions) and the upside is 1,300 (higher conversion or repeat purchase). Each label should list the assumption that differs from the base.
