Creating an Entrepreneurial Venture

Strong ventures start from a recurring problem, not a fashionable technology. This unit takes you from where ideas come from, through generating, screening and developing them into products, to choosing a legal form and setting up and registering a small enterprise.

Remember two separations. Idea generation (divergent) is different from idea selection (convergent). A prototype, an MVP and a market-ready product serve different purposes.

Case bank

Short Indian cases to quote in answers. Name the case, state what it shows, and link it to the concept.

  • Krishigati Pvt. Ltd.

    Builds self-propelled electric agricultural tool bars for marginal farmers that cover sowing, weeding, spraying and soil work.

    Use it for: Creative problem solving. The venture tackles farm operations, labour shortage, affordability and usability together (II.3).

  • Canectar Foods Pvt. Ltd. (CaneBOT)

    Moved from sugarcane juice kiosks to an automated, hygienic robotic vending machine.

    Use it for: Product planning as learning. It shows a pivot, an MVP and patent thinking, with hygiene and automation added after market learning (II.4).

  • Eeki Automation Pvt. Ltd.

    Uses under-utilised land and service and farm alliances for pesticide-free vegetable production.

    Use it for: Sources of ideas such as local resources, sustainability and new technology, and also alliances (II.1, II.7).

  • Autocracy Machinery Pvt. Ltd.

    Builds specialised attachments such as trenchers, pole diggers, ditchers and multi-utility power packs using a Made-in-India supply chain.

    Use it for: Small-enterprise setup and registration. Machinery, supply chain, field service and registration discipline all matter (II.8, II.9).

2-mark questions with model answers

Write your answer first. A 2-mark answer needs a precise definition or two clear points, not a paragraph.

Define a business idea.

A business idea is a possible product, service or venture concept that responds to a customer problem or need. It is a starting point that still has to be tested for need, feasibility and value.

State the meaning of a business opportunity.

A business opportunity is a favourable, evidence-backed situation in which a defined customer has a meaningful need, and a venture can feasibly create and capture value by meeting it. It is an idea plus evidence of need, a payer and feasibility.

List any two sources of business ideas.

(1) Customer complaints and unmet needs, and (2) research outcomes, patents and technical papers. Personal experience, industrial visits and internships, policy and sustainability trends, and local resources are also valid.

Name any two methods of generating business ideas.

(1) Brainstorming and (2) SCAMPER. Mind mapping, customer interviews, observation studies, problem trees, design thinking and competitor analysis are also valid.

Define brainstorming.

Brainstorming is a group idea-generation method in which participants produce as many ideas as possible on a clearly stated problem, postpone criticism and build on each other's suggestions. Screening happens in a separate later stage.

Name the three product-selection stages shown in the textbook figure.

Aurangabadkar and Singh (Fig. 2.3): (1) idea generation, (2) evaluation (screening and pre-feasibility study) and (3) choice.

List any two criteria used to select a product or venture.

(1) Market demand and marketability, and (2) technical feasibility with available technology and skills. Availability of funds, raw materials, qualified personnel, profitability and government policy are also valid.

State the meaning of creative problem solving.

Creative problem solving is a disciplined process of defining the real problem, gathering information, finding root causes, generating alternatives, selecting a solution and testing it, alternating between divergent and convergent thinking.

Distinguish a symptom from a root cause.

A symptom is the visible effect of a problem, such as low sales. A root cause is the underlying reason it occurs, such as distrust, poor fit or high price. Fixing the root cause removes the symptom, while fixing a symptom does not.

State the meaning of product planning.

Product planning is the process of converting a selected opportunity into a product concept, technical specifications, prototype, test plan, launch plan and improvement cycle, balancing what users want with what can be built profitably and responsibly.

State the meaning of a minimum viable product.

A minimum viable product (MVP) is the smallest coherent version of an offering that lets a target customer experience the core value and gives the venture real market learning with limited effort.

Define a prototype.

A prototype is an early, often incomplete representation of a product built to answer a specific learning question about form, function, usability or performance.

Define sole proprietorship.

A sole proprietorship is a business owned, controlled and managed by a single person who receives all profits and bears all risks with unlimited liability. The business has no legal identity separate from its owner.

List four factors used to choose a legal form for a venture.

(1) Capital needs and plans for outside investment, (2) liability exposure, (3) governance, control and number of owners, and (4) compliance burden and continuity. Tax treatment and growth plans are also valid.

Define partnership.

A partnership is a business owned by two or more persons who agree to share profits, responsibilities and risks under an agreement (the partnership deed). It is governed by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, and partners usually have unlimited liability.

State the meaning of a Limited Liability Partnership.

A Limited Liability Partnership (LLP) is a business form under the LLP Act, 2008 that is a separate legal entity with perpetual succession, where partners manage the business and each partner's liability is limited to the agreed contribution.

List four matters normally covered by a founder agreement.

(1) Equity ownership and vesting, (2) roles and decision rights, (3) intellectual-property ownership, and (4) exit, deadlock and dispute resolution. Capital contribution, salary and confidentiality are also valid.

State the meaning of a private limited company.

A private limited company is a company incorporated under the Companies Act, 2013 that is a separate legal entity with limited liability and perpetual succession. It restricts the transfer of shares and has at most 200 members, and it is the common form for investment-ready startups.

Define franchising.

Franchising is a business arrangement in which a franchisor grants a franchisee the right to operate using its brand, products, systems and support, in return for fees and royalties and compliance with set standards.

Name any two routes for transferring intellectual property to a startup.

(1) Assignment, which transfers ownership, and (2) licensing (exclusive or non-exclusive), which permits use while the owner keeps ownership. A joint venture and a spin-off are also valid.

List any two steps in setting up a small enterprise.

(1) Select the project and confirm demand through a feasibility study, and (2) prepare the project report and arrange finance. Choosing the legal form and location, registering and obtaining approvals, and arranging machinery and staff are also valid.

State the purpose of Udyam Registration.

Udyam Registration gives an eligible micro, small or medium enterprise a formal MSME identity and classification through the free official Government of India portal. That identity enables access to MSME schemes, credit support, procurement preferences and delayed-payment protection.

14-mark questions

Plan before you write: an introduction, the points below as headings with explanation and an example, then a short conclusion. Open a question to see the points to cover.

1Explain the sources, methods and screening criteria used to identify a business idea.

Points to cover

  • Sources such as personal pain points, customer complaints, research and patents, industrial exposure, policy and sustainability trends, and local resources.
  • Methods such as brainstorming, SCAMPER, mind mapping, interviews, observation, problem trees and design thinking.
  • Separate divergent generation from convergent screening.
  • Screening criteria are market demand, funds, raw materials, technical feasibility, profitability, personnel, government policy and corporate objectives.
  • Product selection runs idea generation → evaluation (pre-feasibility) → choice.
  • Example such as the hostel energy-saving ideas narrowed to one feasible option.

Study: II.1 Sources of new ideas, II.2 Methods of generating ideas

2Explain product planning from problem definition to pilot release.

Points to cover

  • Define the problem and target user, and state the value proposition.
  • Identify assumptions and requirements, then develop and screen concepts.
  • Select an architecture, build a prototype and test technical and user performance.
  • Estimate cost, capacity and manufacturability.
  • Build an MVP, then run a controlled pilot with metrics and a decision date.
  • Each gate needs evidence. Balance desirability, feasibility, viability and responsibility, using an example such as the posture monitor.

Study: II.4 Product planning and development process

3Explain the features of sole proprietorship, partnership, LLP and private limited company.

Points to cover

  • Sole proprietorship has one owner, no separate entity, unlimited liability, low compliance and continuity tied to the owner.
  • Partnership has two or more partners under the Partnership Act 1932 and a deed, with unlimited joint and several liability and an optional registration.
  • LLP falls under the LLP Act 2008, with a separate entity, limited liability, perpetual succession and MCA filings.
  • Private limited company falls under the Companies Act 2013, with a separate entity, limited liability, shares, 2 to 200 members and the highest compliance.
  • Compare ownership, liability, continuity, capital raising and compliance in a table.
  • Conclude with which form suits which venture.

Study: II.5 Sole proprietorship, II.6 Partnership and Limited Liability Partnership, II.7 Corporation and franchising

4Explain the franchise model, including brand support, fees, quality control and restrictions.

Points to cover

  • Define the franchisor and the franchisee and the contractual right to use the brand and system.
  • Support from the franchisor includes brand, operating manual, training, technology, approved suppliers and marketing.
  • Fees and costs are the initial franchise fee, ongoing royalty, marketing contribution and working capital.
  • Quality control covers standards, audits and corrective action rights. Localisation must be governed.
  • Restrictions cover territory, suppliers, pricing and renewal or exit terms.
  • Benefits and risks include lower entry uncertainty against brand dependency. Speak to existing franchisees before signing.

Study: II.7 Corporation and franchising

5Describe the purpose and main clauses of a founder agreement.

Points to cover

  • The purpose is to turn informal promises into governance before pressure makes disputes personal.
  • Clauses on roles, decision rights and time commitment.
  • Clauses on capital contribution, equity ownership and vesting.
  • Clauses on salary, expenses and additional funding.
  • Clauses on IP assignment and confidentiality.
  • Clauses on deadlock, conflict, founder exit, share transfer restrictions and dispute resolution.

Study: II.6 Partnership and Limited Liability Partnership

6Explain a stage-gated product-development process and the evidence expected at each stage.

Points to cover

  • A stage gate is a decision point to continue, change, pause or stop.
  • The problem stage needs evidence of a frequent, painful problem.
  • The concept stage needs user reaction and screening scores.
  • The prototype stage needs technical performance and usability results.
  • The MVP and pilot stages need adoption, willingness to pay and pilot metrics.
  • The launch stage needs reliability, cost, supply, compliance and service readiness. Commitment grows as uncertainty falls.

7Explain the difference between an MVP, a prototype and a market-ready product.

Points to cover

  • A prototype answers a learning question about form, function, usability or performance and may not be saleable.
  • An MVP is the smallest coherent offering a real customer can use to experience the core value.
  • A market-ready product adds reliability, quality, documentation, support, compliance, supply and viable cost.
  • Compare purpose, audience, completeness and evidence produced in a table.
  • Use an example such as CaneBOT's progression.
  • Treating them as the same thing causes launch failures.

Study: II.4 Product planning and development process

8Describe the sequence for setting up a small product or service enterprise.

Points to cover

  • Select the project, covering product or service, location, feasibility study and business plan.
  • Decide the legal form (constitution).
  • Prepare the project report with market, process, investment, working capital and profitability.
  • Arrange finance, land or premises, machinery, utilities and manpower.
  • Register (Udyam, GST and so on) and obtain clearances.
  • Start operations and monitor. Avoid buying machinery before demand is confirmed (example the 3D printing centre).

Study: II.8 Setup process of small scale enterprise, II.9 Registration of small scale industry

9(i) Explain how observation and interviews help frame a recurring customer problem.(ii) List four venture concepts that could address a laboratory waiting-time problem.

Points to cover

  • Observation reveals actual behaviour, workarounds and delays.
  • Interviews reveal motives, priorities and the user's own language.
  • Combine them with operational data on frequency and cost, then write a problem statement naming user, context, need and evidence.
  • Concepts include a sample-tracking app with status alerts, a digital token and queue system, priority triage tagging, an automated sample hand-off or barcode workflow, and scheduled time slots.

Study: II.1 Sources of new ideas, II.3 Creative problem solving

10(i) Explain the features of a traditional partnership and an LLP.(ii) Describe the circumstances in which each form may be suitable.

Points to cover

  • Partnership features are the 1932 Act, a deed, unlimited joint liability, simplicity and optional registration.
  • LLP features are the 2008 Act, a separate entity, limited liability, perpetual succession and MCA compliance.
  • A partnership suits small, low-risk, trust-based businesses where simplicity matters.
  • An LLP suits professional or consulting firms with client contracts and liability exposure, or where continuity matters.

Study: II.6 Partnership and Limited Liability Partnership

11(i) Explain the purpose of a product roadmap.(ii) Describe how customer value, evidence, risk and dependency guide feature priority.

Points to cover

  • A roadmap is a time-phased path from present evidence to a target customer and business outcome, not a wish list.
  • Its layers are outcome, assumption, capability, release and review.
  • Features essential to core value, or that remove a safety or compliance barrier, come first.
  • Features that test the riskiest assumption or unlock dependent work come early.
  • Consider effort, service burden and opportunity cost, but not team popularity. Example, allergen accuracy before cosmetic features.

Study: II.4 Product planning and development process

12(i) Explain the benefits and limitations of franchising a technical-training model.(ii) Describe methods for maintaining curriculum quality and local relevance.

Points to cover

  • Benefits are faster expansion with the franchisee's capital, local market knowledge and a proven curriculum.
  • Limitations are quality dilution, dependence on franchisees, brand risk and royalty disputes.
  • Quality methods include trainer certification, a controlled curriculum, standard assessments and periodic audits.
  • Local relevance comes from governed localisation of examples, language and schedule, without changing outcomes.
  • Monitor learner outcomes and allow corrective action.

Study: II.7 Corporation and franchising

13(i) Explain the features of an LLP and a private limited company.(ii) Describe how the legal form affects governance and future financing.

Points to cover

  • Both are separate entities with limited liability and perpetual succession.
  • An LLP is managed by partners through an agreement and has lighter compliance.
  • A private limited company has shares, a board and more compliance.
  • Governance differs, with the LLP agreement setting decision rights and the company using a board, shareholders and articles.
  • Equity investors and ESOPs need shares, so a private limited company is preferred for VC-funded growth.

Study: II.6 Partnership and Limited Liability Partnership, II.7 Corporation and franchising

14(i) Describe the categories of registration and compliance for a small enterprise.(ii) Explain the difference among business registration, MSME identity, tax, local, labour and sector requirements.

Points to cover

  • Entity formation creates the legal form (company, LLP or registered partnership).
  • MSME identity through Udyam gives classification and scheme access.
  • Tax covers GST, income tax and TDS where applicable.
  • Local covers trade licence and shop or establishment registration.
  • Labour covers EPF, ESI and factory licence where applicable. Sector covers FSSAI, pollution consent and product standards.
  • One registration does not replace another. Verify against current official sources.

Study: II.9 Registration of small scale industry, II.8 Setup process of small scale enterprise

15(i) Describe how manufacturability, serviceability, cost and customer evidence are considered during product planning.(ii) Explain why a technically successful prototype may still be unsuitable for market release.

Points to cover

  • Design for manufacture, meaning process, tolerances, suppliers and batch economics.
  • Serviceability, meaning spare parts, repair and field support.
  • Set cost targets early, before the design is frozen.
  • Customer evidence such as pilot use and willingness to pay guides features.
  • A prototype proves only that it can work. It may lack reliability, cost viability, compliance, supply or support, as with the soil sensor that farmers rejected.
